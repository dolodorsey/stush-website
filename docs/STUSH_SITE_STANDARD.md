# STUSH Digital Flagship Standard

Status: LOCKED PRODUCTION STANDARD
Owner: STUSH
Purpose: make every release, page, product, campaign and interaction feel like one premium fashion house while protecting commerce clarity, real brand assets, speed and visual QA.

## 1. Non-negotiable homepage hero rules

1. The homepage landing canvas is a visual experience first: campaign video, animation or approved cinematic sequence.
2. Never place headlines, body copy, CTAs, badges, captions, labels, counters, prices, navigation prompts or marketing text on top of the homepage animation.
3. The primary navigation and announcement bar may remain above the landing canvas as site chrome; they are not part of the animation artwork.
4. The hero canvas must not contain hidden overlay copy that appears only at certain breakpoints.
5. The hero canvas must not contain text baked into a generated visual unless that text is part of an approved STUSH source asset.
6. Homepage copy begins in the next section after the animation ends.
7. The first post-hero section must create an obvious visual break from the animation and establish the editorial story.
8. The hero media must cover the available frame without stretching, letterboxing or exposing empty browser background.
9. Desktop hero target: 78–92svh. Mobile hero target: 68–82svh unless the source composition requires more.
10. Hero video must autoplay muted, loop, use playsInline and provide an approved poster fallback.
11. The poster frame must look intentional enough to function as a still hero if autoplay is blocked.
12. The hero must remain legible as a visual at 390px, 768px, 1024px, 1440px and 1920px widths.
13. No copy may overlap the hero because of sticky navigation, negative margins or viewport-height bugs.
14. Reduced-motion users must receive a strong still frame or non-distracting alternative.

## 2. Brand world and visual direction

15. STUSH is fashion editorial + Atlanta street intelligence + premium retail, not a generic black-and-gold luxury template.
16. The site may use black, bone/ivory, white, STUSH red and restrained metallic/warm accents. Gold is an accent, never the default color of every section.
17. Black sections should feel cinematic, not empty. Bone/ivory sections should feel editorial, not generic ecommerce.
18. Use contrast rhythm: dark cinematic section -> light commerce/editorial section -> dark story section -> light detail section.
19. Avoid five or more consecutive dark sections with similar spacing and typography.
20. Every major page needs one memorable visual composition that is unique to that page.
21. Do not repeat the same Atlanta skyline, same model crop or same hero composition across pages.
22. Atlanta may appear as origin and attitude, but STUSH cannot visually collapse into an Atlanta tourism campaign.
23. The visual system should support future cities, campaigns, collaborators and global growth without redesigning the core site.
24. Luxury is communicated through proportion, restraint, image quality, typography, interaction and detail—not by adding gold.
25. Editorial asymmetry is encouraged when it improves hierarchy; random misalignment is not.
26. Large negative space is permitted only when it is visibly intentional and balanced by typography, image scale or motion.
27. Use oversized editorial typography selectively. Not every section gets a giant serif headline.
28. Avoid repeating the same headline structure on every page.
29. Red should function as impact punctuation: a word, rule, state or selected interaction—not a blanket background everywhere.
30. Borders should be hairline and architectural. Avoid heavy boxes unless intentionally brutalist.

## 3. Asset integrity

31. Product cards must use the correct real product image mapped to that exact product.
32. Never use a generic, recycled, placeholder, stale or unrelated product image on a commerce card.
33. Never use AI-recreated merchandise as the sellable product image.
34. Approved STUSH logos must be used as supplied. Do not redraw, restyle, warp or invent a logo variation inside the website code.
35. Lifestyle images may be editorial/generated only when clearly used as campaign atmosphere and not as a false product representation.
36. If an editorial visual shows a product, the garment branding and silhouette must not misrepresent the actual product being sold.
37. Prefer real campaign photography and real product photography over generic luxury imagery whenever available.
38. All uploaded campaign assets require a documented destination: homepage hero, collection hero, lookbook, journal, campaign module or social—not a random reuse everywhere.
39. Each image should have one canonical role before secondary reuse.
40. Do not use low-resolution imagery in full-bleed placements.
41. Do not stretch raster logos or product shots beyond their useful resolution.
42. Preserve alpha/transparency on clean logo files.
43. Product photography backgrounds should remain consistent inside a grid unless the layout intentionally creates an editorial break.
44. When product cutouts are used, they must have consistent scale, padding and baseline across the row.

## 4. Merchandising rules

45. The website is a selling environment, not only a mood board.
46. The first commerce section must appear quickly after the cinematic introduction.
47. Product name, price and click target must always be obvious.
48. Product cards must remain product-first. Decorative UI may not obscure the garment.
49. Product grids should default to four columns on large desktop, two on tablet and one or two on mobile depending on image density.
50. Product card image aspect ratio and internal padding must be consistent within a grid.
51. Hover imagery is encouraged only when the second image is valid for the same product.
52. Hover effects must not cause layout shift.
53. Quick actions must never cover critical product details or remain permanently visible over small screens.
54. Do not fabricate prices, availability, colorways, ratings, product counts or inventory states in editorial modules.
55. Product counts must come from live commerce data when displayed.
56. Category cards must link to the exact filtered collection represented by the visual.
57. Category visuals must be sourced from an actual product or approved campaign mapped to that category.
58. New arrivals, current edit, essentials and women should be dynamically merchandised rather than hand-coded with stale SKUs.
59. Sold-out behavior must remain visually premium and clear, never broken or misleading.
60. Product pages must prioritize image, name, price, variant selection, add-to-cart and fit/product information before long narrative copy.

## 5. Homepage architecture

61. Section 1: announcement + navigation + wordless hero animation.
62. Section 2: editorial introduction on a contrasting surface. This is where the campaign headline, statement and primary CTAs begin.
63. Section 3: Current Edit using live real products.
64. Section 4: Shop by Category using image-led category cards, not only a text directory.
65. Section 5: campaign/editorial story with a strong full-width or asymmetrical visual.
66. Section 6: secondary category index or wardrobe architecture for power shoppers.
67. Section 7: brand/world story—Atlanta origin without becoming repetitive city imagery.
68. Section 8: Society/email capture on a clean contrasting background.
69. Footer: commerce, editorial, services and brand/company information with no dead links.
70. The homepage should feel complete before the footer; do not rely on footer content to explain the brand.

## 6. Page-specific minimums

71. Shop page: strong page opening + category/filter navigation + dense premium product grid + one editorial interruption.
72. Collections page: image-led wardrobe architecture + category counts from live data + clear destination hierarchy.
73. Women page: its own art direction and merchandising logic; not the mens/unisex page with a label changed.
74. Essentials page: visual emphasis on fabrication, uniformity, layering and repeat-wear value.
75. Outerwear page: garment construction, texture, hardware and silhouette should dominate imagery.
76. Hoodies & Sweats page: texture, wash, weight, fit and layering should be visually emphasized.
77. Jerseys & Polos / Tees & Tops: sport/culture references may be stronger, but the page must still look STUSH—not a generic sports retailer.
78. Bottoms: include full-body or lower-body styling context when approved imagery exists.
79. Accessories: macro detail photography is preferred because detail is the story.
80. Lookbook: editorial pacing, fewer commerce UI elements, large image sequences, captions only when useful.
81. Journal: story-led cards with real editorial value; do not use journal as filler content.
82. Product detail page: premium gallery, consistent image treatment, sticky purchase information on desktop, clear mobile purchase flow.
83. Cart/checkout handoff: minimal, fast, calm and trustworthy.

## 7. Typography and hierarchy

84. Bodoni/Didot-style serif is the editorial display voice; sans/mono are utility voices.
85. Never use display serif for small functional labels where legibility suffers.
86. Display headlines must be manually line-broken when composition matters.
87. Avoid orphaned one-word lines unless intentionally composed.
88. Headline line-height should remain tight; body line-height should remain generous.
89. Kicker text uses mono/sans uppercase with controlled tracking.
90. Do not use low-contrast gray body text below accessible readability thresholds.
91. Product names must remain highly legible and should not be all-caps unless the design system explicitly calls for it.
92. Prices must never be visually weaker than decorative labels.

## 8. Motion and interaction

93. Motion should feel slow, smooth and editorial—not bouncy, gamified or template-like.
94. Preferred easing: cubic-bezier(.16,1,.3,1) / similarly restrained luxury easing.
95. Hover scale should generally remain below 1.04.
96. Scroll reveal must never leave content permanently invisible if JavaScript fails.
97. Avoid simultaneous motion on every visible object.
98. Product hover transitions should focus on image change, slight lift or subtle crop—not dramatic rotation.
99. Navigation transitions should be fast enough to feel responsive.
100. No interaction may shift the page vertically after user input unless intentionally expanding content.
101. Reduced-motion mode must disable nonessential parallax, marquee and entrance animation.
102. Mobile must not depend on hover to reveal necessary information.

## 9. Responsive standards

103. Design desktop and mobile intentionally; do not merely stack the desktop layout.
104. Test at minimum: 390x844, 430x932, 768x1024, 1024x768, 1440x1100 and 1920x1080.
105. No horizontal overflow at any approved viewport.
106. Sticky elements must account for announcement + navigation height.
107. Category labels may wrap once; if they wrap repeatedly, reduce scale or change layout.
108. Full-bleed media must preserve the subject at mobile crop points.
109. Mobile text must not sit against viewport edges; minimum practical side padding is 16px.
110. Touch targets should be at least 44px in the dimension used for interaction when feasible.
111. Mobile product cards must display name and price without hover.
112. Desktop compositions that rely on extreme asymmetry must collapse into a deliberate mobile sequence.

## 10. Performance standards

113. Hero video should be compressed for web delivery and avoid unnecessary 4K bitrate.
114. Large still images should use responsive Next/Image sizing where possible.
115. Do not eagerly load every image below the fold.
116. Only first-viewport critical images receive priority loading.
117. Keep cumulative layout shift near zero by reserving image/video dimensions.
118. Avoid shipping unused animation libraries for simple effects.
119. Maintain a fast first interaction on mobile connections.
120. Audit any image larger than 1.5 MB unless there is a justified campaign need and an optimized derivative.

## 11. Accessibility and trust

121. Product and editorial images require useful alt text unless purely decorative.
122. Decorative video should be marked appropriately and must not steal screen-reader focus.
123. Keyboard focus must remain visible.
124. Navigation, filters, product links and forms must be usable by keyboard.
125. Form fields require labels or accessible names.
126. Color alone cannot communicate availability or selection.
127. Motion cannot be required to understand navigation or commerce.
128. Links must point to live, intentional destinations; no decorative dead anchors.

## 12. Visual QA before production

129. Every meaningful visual change requires screenshots before it is considered done.
130. Screenshot the actual built website, not only a design mockup.
131. Capture full-page desktop screenshots for: /, /shop, /collections, /lookbook and /journal.
132. Capture mobile screenshots for at least / and /shop.
133. The homepage screenshot must prove the landing animation area contains no marketing copy overlay.
134. Automated DOM QA must verify the hero contains no h1, h2, h3, p, a, button or non-empty text node.
135. Automated QA must verify the post-hero editorial section begins at or below the hero bottom boundary.
136. Automated QA must fail on horizontal page overflow.
137. Automated QA must fail when primary routes return non-200 responses.
138. Review screenshots for clipped typography, blank media, wrong images, stretched products, repeated content, unreadable contrast and accidental whitespace.
139. Review both first viewport and full page; a strong top screen does not excuse weak sections below.
140. Production is not complete until build, route checks, DOM rules and screenshot generation pass.

## 13. Generated-concept guardrails

141. Generated mockups are art-direction references, not literal production assets.
142. Do not copy fake product prices, fake product counts, fake inventory or invented navigation from a mockup.
143. Do not copy AI-invented logos or distorted STUSH marks.
144. Do not replace real merchandise photography with concept garments generated for a mockup.
145. Use the best ideas from mockups: scale, rhythm, framing, contrast, pacing, editorial composition and storytelling.
146. Translate those ideas using approved STUSH assets and live commerce data.
147. When the concept and real asset conflict, the real product/brand asset wins.
148. When a generated layout is visually strong but commercially weak, preserve the composition and repair the commerce hierarchy.
149. When a generated layout becomes repetitive, create a new composition rather than reskinning the same module.
150. STUSH should always look like STUSH, not like an imitation of another fashion house.

## 14. Release gate

A release is production-ready only when all are true:
- Wordless homepage animation rule passes.
- Real product/asset mapping passes.
- Build passes.
- Primary route contract passes.
- Desktop screenshots pass review.
- Mobile screenshots pass review.
- No horizontal overflow.
- Navigation and cart routes work.
- Product data is live and not fabricated.
- No obvious placeholder copy or imagery remains.
- Performance is acceptable for production.
- Any exception is documented before deployment.
